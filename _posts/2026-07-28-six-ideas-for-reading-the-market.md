News always arrives in pieces. One day there is a story saying chip makers just posted their best profits ever. The same day, another story says their stock dropped sharply. The pieces do not explain each other.

People who have watched markets for a long time tie those pieces together with a handful of ideas. An idea is not a forecast. Forecasts are often wrong. But a good idea tells you what went wrong and why, even when the forecast misses. Below are six ideas that were actually used to explain the market in the first half of 2026.

---

## 1. The rate on paper is not the real rate

A policy rate of 3.75 percent sounds high. But if prices rose 3.8 percent over the past year, the story changes. Someone who borrowed money pays 3.75 percent in interest, while the value of what they owe shrinks by 3.8 percent. The rate they actually paid is below zero.

Take the stated rate and subtract inflation, and what is left is called the real interest rate. In April 2026 the United States had a policy rate of 3.75 percent against inflation of 3.8 percent, so this number turned negative for the first time. Korea saw the same thing, with a base rate of 2.5 percent against inflation of 2.6 percent.

Why does the sign matter? A central bank raises rates to slow the economy down. If the real rate is negative, then no matter how high the stated rate looks, the economy is not being slowed. It is also a sign that prices still have room to climb.

---

## 2. The neutral rate gives you a line to measure against

How do you know whether a rate is high or low? You need something to compare it to. That something is the neutral rate. It is the level that neither pushes the economy forward nor holds it back.

The Fed puts its own neutral rate near 3.1 percent. Take away the 2 percent inflation target and you get 1.1 percent. That is the line for the United States in real terms.

Now there are two numbers to watch. One is the real rate, and the other is that 1.1 percent line. If the real rate sits above the line, policy is still tight, so there is room to cut. If it has dropped below the line, money is already loose and there is no case for cutting further.

In April 2026 the real rate in the United States was about minus 0.05 percent, far below the line. This is a way of judging rate cuts by two numbers instead of by politics or pressure from a president. Whether or not you agree with the conclusion, the strength of the method is that anyone can go check the numbers it rests on.

---

## 3. There is more than one way for a market to stall

There are two ways a market can stop going up.

In the first, prices barely move but time passes. The market drifts sideways. It is tiring, but you do not lose much.

In the second, prices actually fall. Both get called a pause, and they are completely different to live through.

This is not a matter of wording. If you expect the first kind, you sit still. If it turns out to be the second kind, you cut your position. The response is the opposite.

A forecast made in early June 2026 said the market would rest over the summer, but that it would be the first kind. What happened instead was that the Kospi closed above 9,000 on June 18 and then fell to 6,500 in July. The direction was right and the size was wrong. An idea can be useful and still fail to tell you how hard the drop will be.

---

## 4. Was it earnings that lifted the index, or was it money?

People explain the same rally in different ways. One side says the index rose because chip makers made a lot of money. The other side says it rose because there was more cash floating around.

The second side explains it with a snack. You can love a certain snack, but you cannot buy it with an empty wallet. When you do have money, you buy the snack you already like first. A good industry will not show up in stock prices if there is no money in the market, and when money does arrive it goes first to the places everyone already knows are good.

One further observation here is worth remembering. When the United States put tariffs on other countries, those countries tried to soften the blow by cutting rates or spending more. That put more money into the world, and some of it found its way into stocks. If that chain holds, then the conclusion is this. If the tariffs go away, the flood of money could go away with them.

That runs against common sense, which is why it is worth filing away. Something that looks like bad news can work as good news through a different path, and the habit of tracing that path matters.

---

## 5. A growth rate and an actual amount are two different things

On July 7, 2026, Samsung Electronics reported the best quarter in its history. Revenue was 171 trillion won and operating profit was 89.4 trillion won, both records. The stock fell almost 7 percent that day. SK Hynix fell about 6 percent alongside it, and both dropped again the next day.

Record results and a falling stock look like a contradiction until you look at what comes next. Analyst estimates at the time put Samsung's operating profit for 2026 at about 362 trillion won, more than seven times what it earned the year before. For 2027 the estimate was about 499 trillion won. For 2028 it turned into a small decline, to about 469 trillion won.

Watch what happens across those years. In 2028 the company is still earning close to a record amount. What changes is the speed. Going from about 44 trillion won to 362 trillion won is a jump of more than 700 percent. Going from 362 to 499 adds another 137 trillion won of profit, more than the company used to make in an entire year, and yet it reads as only 38 percent growth. Nothing broke. The number being compared against simply got much bigger.

So why does the stock fall? Two things happen at once.

The first is the discount rate. A stock price is not this year's profit. It is all the money the company will earn in the future, turned into what that money is worth today. When rates go up, money that arrives far in the future gets cut down more. The same profit is simply worth less.

The second reason runs deeper. In an industry that moves in cycles, the real question is not how much a company earns but how long it keeps earning it. The market puts its own guess about that length into the stock price every single day. A falling stock means the market has started to assume a shorter run of good years than it did before.

---

## 6. Having a calendar

To answer the question of how long, you need a calendar. Two of them were used.

The first is the business cycle. As of March 2026 the United States had been expanding for 71 months. Since expansions have lasted about 7 years and 1 month on average since 1980, the expectation was that this one would run until June 2027. That gives the rally a deadline.

The second is the life span of a technology. The internet boom lasted about 18 years and the mobile boom about 15. By that measure the AI boom would run roughly 12 years, out to 2034. The boom then gets split into a first half and a second half. In the first half, American firms have not built their plants yet, so Korean chip makers take the benefit. As the second half arrives, American firms win that share back.

There is a trap in building a calendar this way. An average changes a lot depending on which years you count. The average American expansion since 1980 is 85 months if you include the short one that ran from 1980 to 1981, and 103 months if you leave it out. That is a gap of a year and a half. Whenever someone hands you an average, ask what it is an average of.

---

## Ideas are not answers. They are a way of asking

Here are the six ideas once more. The real rate is what is left after you take out inflation, and you can only call it high or low by holding it up against a neutral line. A market that stalls can stall in more than one way, and earnings are not the only thing that moves an index. A slower growth rate is not the same as shrinking profit. And to answer how long, you need a calendar.

The forecasts built on these ideas came out about half right. The call that the Bank of Korea would raise rates landed. The call that a pause was coming in summer got the direction right. But the size of the drop was badly off, and the call for a Fed cut in the fourth quarter was the opposite of what Fed officials themselves expected in June.

The ideas still hold up, because they let you point at where the miss happened. Without the notion of a real rate, the question of why prices keep rising when the policy rate is 3.75 percent never even comes to mind. Without the split between a market that drifts and a market that drops, hearing that the market will rest tells you nothing about what to do.

Reading a market is less about knowing the answer and more about holding a better question.
